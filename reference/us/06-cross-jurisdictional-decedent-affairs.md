# 06 — Cross-jurisdictional decedent affairs (US state-to-state)

Common in US online-funeral usage: the family is in one state, the deceased lived in another, the death occurred in yet a third. The compass cites this file when state-to-state issues come up.

**Out of scope (different compass file or no compass file):** family in US, deceased in another country → partially covered by the "Empty-country handling" gate in [rules.md](../../rules.md); refer to State Department and consulate. The Funeral Consumers Alliance "Death Away From Home" guide at [funerals.org/get-help/death-away-from-home/](https://www.funerals.org/get-help/death-away-from-home/) is the most useful US-side starting resource.

## Three common cross-state scenarios

### Scenario A — Family in State X, deceased lived and died in State Y

The most common case. The deceased was a snowbird, lived in a retirement state, traveled, or simply lived in a different state from the rest of the family.

- **State of death (Y) issues the death certificate.** The family orders certificates from Y's vital records office, not their own state's.
- **Probate is opened in the state where the deceased was domiciled.** Usually this is also Y, but not always — if the deceased was a long-term Florida resident who died on a Massachusetts vacation, probate is still Florida.
- **The funeral home in State Y** handles disposition (cremation or burial in Y, OR shipment to State X for burial — see Scenario B).
- **The family in State X** handles probate filings and account closures by mail / online / phone with State Y's institutions and probate court — they may not need to travel to State Y at all, though one trip is often helpful.

### Scenario B — Body needs to be moved from State Y (death) to State X (burial)

- **Embalming is required for interstate transport** by most airlines and many state regulations (one of the rare cases where embalming is genuinely required).
- **A Transit Permit / Burial-Transit Permit** is issued by State Y's vital records office, often via the funeral director.
- **The receiving funeral home in State X** coordinates with the State Y funeral home — this is routine for funeral directors; both will know what's needed.
- **Cost:** $1,500–$5,000+ for the transit, depending on distance, airline vs. ground transport, and which funeral homes are involved. Get an itemized estimate before authorizing.
- **Timeline:** Typically adds 2–5 days to the timeline.

### Scenario C — Family in State X, deceased's accounts/assets in multiple states

- **Ancillary probate** may be required in each state where the deceased held real property. Primary probate is in the state of domicile; ancillary probate handles the out-of-state real estate.
- **For non-real-estate accounts** (banks, brokerages, retirement accounts) — usually handled by mail/online with a certified death certificate from the state of death and a "letter of testamentary" or "letter of administration" from the primary probate court.
- **A probate attorney in the deceased's state of domicile** is the right starting point. They'll coordinate with attorneys in other states if ancillary probate is needed.

## State variations that matter

| Topic | Variation | What the compass should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Probate-required estate size** | Varies $30K–$200K+ — many states have "small estate" thresholds that avoid full probate | "Ask the probate attorney whether your state's small-estate option applies — it can save months." |
| **Cremation authorization** | Some states require all next-of-kin to sign; some require only one with priority | "The funeral home in the state of death knows their state's rules." |
| **Transit permit requirements** | Some states require death certificate first; others issue with provisional cause of death | "The funeral home handles this; ask for an estimate of timeline." |
| **Notarization for out-of-state filings** | Some banks require their forms notarized; some accept e-notarization | "Find a notary in your home state — banks/UPS Stores/your bank usually offer this for free." |
| **Will deposit requirement** | Some states require the will be filed with probate court within a window (CA: 30 days; many states: similar) | "Filing the will is NOT the same as opening probate — it can be done without an attorney; opening probate usually needs one." |

## The "where do I open probate" question

**Answer:** in the state and county where the deceased was **domiciled** (lived, intended to live as their primary home, had their drivers license / voter registration / homestead).

NOT in:
- The state where the family lives (unless the deceased lived there too)
- The state where the death occurred (unless that's also the domicile state)
- The state where the deceased owned a vacation home (unless that's their primary)

The family does NOT need to physically travel to the probate court — most filings are by mail or online, an attorney handles the in-court appearances. **One trip during probate** is sometimes useful for sorting belongings and meeting the attorney; not strictly required.

## When the deceased had property in multiple states

- **Real estate:** ancillary probate in each state with property. Some states have streamlined ancillary processes; some don't.
- **Vehicles:** title transferred via the state DMV where the vehicle is registered. A certified death certificate + a small-estate affidavit (in many states) handles this without probate.
- **Bank accounts / brokerages:** typically transferred via the institution's internal process — letters of testamentary from the primary probate court usually suffice. Some accounts had a "transfer on death" (TOD) or "payable on death" (POD) designation — those bypass probate entirely.
- **Retirement accounts (401(k), IRA):** named beneficiaries control; do NOT go through probate. The custodian's claim process is what the family follows.
- **Life insurance:** named beneficiaries control; do NOT go through probate. The insurer's claim process is what the family follows.

## What to STOP before doing

- **STOP** before signing a transfer-on-death affidavit you got in the mail from a brokerage if you haven't talked to the probate attorney — these can affect probate inventories.
- **STOP** before paying a bill of the deceased from your own funds — those become creditor claims against the estate, but only if filed correctly.
- **STOP** before selling out-of-state real estate without checking ancillary probate requirements.
- **STOP** before moving funds out of the deceased's accounts even if you have joint access — joint-with-rights-of-survivorship is fine, but other joint accounts have tax and probate implications.

## Sample family script for the funeral home in another state

> "We're in [State X]. Our [relationship] died in [State Y] on [date]. We need to [cremate / bury / ship remains to State X]. What does the timeline look like, what paperwork do you handle vs. what's on us, and what's an itemized estimate?"
